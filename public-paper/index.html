How often do AI incidents appear in securities filings?

1. How often do public companies identify documented AI incidents in SEC filings?

Among 307 AIID-recorded, issuer-matchable incidents involving 21 U.S.-listed companies, four (1.3%; 95% CI 0.4–3.3) received incident-specific disclosure in Forms 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q during the next 12 months. The percentage is conditional on the study's incident-selection and filing-search rules, not a rate for all corporate AI incidents.

2. What proportion of AI incidents have any substantive securities disclosure?

Seven of 307 incidents (2.3%; 95% CI 0.9–4.6) received either incident-specific disclosure (T1) or disclosure of a directly related legal consequence (T2). The authors call T1+T2 an upper-bound substantive measure because classification of legal-consequence-only cases is less secure than direct incident identification.

3. What is the AI incident disclosure shadow?

The study defines the shadow as incidents without an identifiable incident-specific disclosure or directly related legal-consequence disclosure in the selected filings. It contains 300 of 307 analyzed incidents (97.7%; 95% CI 95.4–99.1). It does not mean the incidents were concealed, legally material, or required to be disclosed.

4. Do general AI risk factors identify the actual AI incidents a company experienced?

Usually not in this sample. Of 307 incidents, 278 had some generic risk language matching an AI application or harm family, but that text generally did not identify the event. Specific incident disclosure was observed for only four incidents.

5. How much of the filing language is weakly related to the underlying AI incident?

Of the 278 incidents coded T3, 84 had language matching both the AI application and harm families (T3a); 194 matched only one family (T3b). With 22 T4 incidents showing no related language, 216 of 307 (70.4%; 95% CI 64.9–75.4) had at most weakly related language.

How can AI incident disclosure be measured?

6. How can an external AI incident database be linked to financial disclosures?

The authors begin with the AI Incident Database snapshot dated July 27, 2026, retain 1,389 incidents dated 2019–2026, identify developers and deployers, and conservatively map organizations to U.S.-listed domestic periodic-report filers. That yields 307 incidents involving 21 issuers for filing searches.

7. Why is incident-level analysis different from counting AI mentions in annual reports?

Counting AI-related terms measures disclosure of a general topic. The study instead starts with an independently recorded event and tests whether it appears in a matched issuer's subsequent 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q. These are distinct measures of transparency.

8. What disclosure taxonomy distinguishes event-specific reporting from boilerplate AI risk language?

The pre-registered taxonomy uses T1 for an identifiable incident, T2 for a directly related legal consequence without identifying the incident, T3 for generic same-family risk language, and T4 when no related language is located. The strongest matching tier within the observation window determines the code.

9. Why distinguish generic AI-risk language that matches two harm families from language matching only one?

The post-hoc T3 relatedness split separates T3a (matching the incident's AI application and harm families) from T3b (matching only one). The distinction shows that even generic coverage varies in relevance. It supplements, but does not replace, the pre-registered four-tier analysis.

10. What observation window was used to find disclosures after AI incidents?

The primary design searched Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q for 12 months after each incident. The authors also examined 6-, 18- and 24-month windows and complete-follow-up cases. A 12-month window is a defined measurement choice, not a universal legal reporting deadline.

Severity, issuer concentration and heterogeneity

11. Are severe AI incidents more likely to appear in securities filings?

The authors found no statistically detectable severity gradient in exploratory comparisons of 42 severe, 50 moderate and 215 limited incidents. This null finding is not proof of equal rates: only seven cases met the substantive-disclosure criterion, leaving the severity test underpowered.

12. How much do a few large firms dominate matched AI incident samples?

The 307-incident sample is concentrated in a few issuers: Alphabet accounts for 86 incidents, Meta for 81, Tesla for 36 and Microsoft for 35. Researchers should account for this concentration rather than treating incidents as independent observations from a broad cross-section of companies.

13. Does the high disclosure-shadow rate depend entirely on Alphabet and Meta?

No. The primary shadow estimate is 97.7%. Dropping the two largest incident contributors gives 96.4% among 140 incidents; dropping the five largest yields 92.5% among 53 incidents. These sensitivity results show the qualitative finding is not solely a top-issuer artifact, though the smaller samples are less precise.

14. Does a firm's AI developer or deployer role explain whether an incident is disclosed?

The apparent association with issuer role is not stable to specification and single-case changes. Developer, deployer-operator, dual-role, platform-venue and mentioned-only attributions reflect different relationships to the event; the authors do not establish a causal role effect.

15. What financial consequences characterize AI incidents that do enter SEC filings?

The seven substantively disclosed cases tended to involve financial-statement effects or formal legal and regulatory consequences. Two examples discussed in the paper are roughly $304 million in Zillow charges associated with winding down algorithmic home buying and a $478 million General Motors Cruise restructuring adjustment. These examples do not establish a disclosure threshold or causal mechanism.

Issuer matching and validation

16. How reliable is linking AI incident records to publicly listed issuers?

The study audited issuer matching with an independent coder on 80 deliberately challenging incidents. Agreement on issuer identity was 82.5% (Cohen's kappa 0.79). Disagreements were concentrated in multi-candidate cases, underscoring the importance of recording matching decisions.

17. How should studies handle incidents involving multiple AI developers, platforms or deployers?

The authors document a deterministic one-issuer selection rule and compare alternative tie-breaks, including preferring a developer or listed parent. Twenty-seven incidents had at least two listed-issuer candidates. The method is conservative but cannot resolve every question of responsibility or corporate ownership.

18. What does the issuer-attribution classification capture beyond developer versus deployer?

The post-hoc attribution classification distinguishes developer, deployer-operator, both, platform-venue and mentioned-only roles. In the primary sample, there were 49 developer-only, 14 deployer-operator, 222 both, 14 platform-venue and eight mentioned-only incidents. Those categories characterize associations in incident records, not legal responsibility.

19. How was reliability of incident-to-filing disclosure coding assessed?

An independent second coding pass covered 68 cases, oversampling rare T1, T2 and T4 outcomes. It achieved 85.3% raw agreement and Cohen's kappa of 0.73. Because substantive disclosure is rare, the article also examines the fragile boundary between T2 and T3 rather than treating every tier as equally certain.

20. How can a study verify that filing keyword searches did not miss incident disclosures?

The authors used a search-independent full-filing retrieval audit and retained filing-level search evidence. The workflow records forms, dates, relevant passages and alternative queries. Retrieval error remains possible, particularly when issuers describe a technology or harm without naming the event.

Materiality, regulation and investor information

21. Does an AI incident's absence from SEC filings establish a securities-law violation?

No. The paper makes no legal materiality or compliance determination for any named issuer or incident. An AIID-recorded event can be immaterial, may lack a disclosure duty, or may be reported in channels outside Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q.

22. How does the disclosure of AI incidents differ from cybersecurity incident disclosure?

The authors use cybersecurity literature as a design analogy: an external event record can be compared with subsequent filings. But material cyber incidents have a specific U.S. reporting architecture, including Form 8-K Item 1.05, whereas the study period had no analogous general U.S. AI-incident reporting duty.

23. What evidence is available on the information gap between AI-related harms and SEC risk-factor disclosures?

This paper contrasts externally documented incidents with the content of matched securities filings. Although generic risk discussion was common, 300 of 307 events lacked identifiable incident-specific or directly related legal-consequence disclosure under the defined protocol. That is an information-visibility finding, not proof of unlawful withholding.

24. Why might firms describe general AI risks without naming particular realized incidents?

Potential explanations include materiality assessment, uncertainty, event attribution, reputational and proprietary considerations, and the different purposes of risk-factor versus incident reporting. The study observes the disclosure pattern but does not identify which explanation caused any specific issuer's wording.

25. Can AI incident disclosure research inform future evaluation of AI reporting regimes?

Yes. The 2019–2026 linked-incident design provides a pre-regime reference point that later studies could repeat as rules evolve, including serious-incident reporting under the EU AI Act. The study does not itself test the effects of the EU AI Act or future U.S. requirements.

Reproducibility, robustness and research limits

26. Is there an openly reproducible dataset linking AI incidents to U.S. securities filings?

The authors provide frozen incident-to-issuer mappings, coding records and a deterministic analysis workflow in the public research repository, with a separately archived Code Ocean artifact. The article DOI should be cited for the research findings; source data from AIID retain applicable attribution and reuse conditions.

27. How sensitive is the 97.7% disclosure-shadow rate to changes in the study design?

The production-approved structured abstract reports shadow estimates from 92.5% to 99.0% across sensitivity specifications. Examples include excluding the largest incident contributors, changing the filing window, and using corrected issuer mappings. These checks do not eliminate selection bias from the incident database.

28. What are the main selection limits of AIID-based corporate disclosure studies?

An incident must be publicly recorded in AIID, contain an identifiable organization, and be resolvable to an eligible U.S.-listed domestic filer. Media visibility and entity matching therefore determine inclusion. The 307-incidence results must not be generalized to all corporate AI failures.

29. Can incident-severity or firm-role regression establish causes of AI disclosure?

Not in this data. Only seven incidents receive T1 or T2, making richer models unstable, and the relevant severity and role comparisons are exploratory. The results support descriptive comparisons, not causal inference or proof of no underlying association.

30. How could future researchers extend this incident-to-filing framework?

Possible extensions include additional investor communication channels, new incident cohorts, market reactions, different geographies and periods after mandatory reporting changes. These are future research directions; the current paper examines three SEC form types and a selected incident population.

AI failures and what investors can see

31. Do companies report the AI failures that appear in the news?

Some externally documented AI incidents become identifiable in securities filings, but the study found direct event-specific wording for only four of 307 matched incidents. It looked specifically at three SEC form types during 12 months, so this is not a count of all media statements or company communications.

32. Can investors learn about real AI harms by reading companies' annual reports?

Annual and current reports can describe consequences and AI risks, but a generic warning may not identify the particular event. In this sample, 97.7% of incidents did not have identifiable incident-specific or related legal-consequence disclosure in the specified forms.

33. Why do companies discuss AI risks in general terms rather than specific cases?

Risk-factor sections serve a different purpose from incident logs, and disclosure obligations can depend on materiality and context. This study documents the gap between general language and named events; it does not determine why any particular company chose specific words.

34. How often are real-world AI incidents mentioned in 10-K or 8-K filings?

In a sample of 307 AIID-recorded incidents linked to listed domestic issuers, four received incident-specific disclosure across Forms 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q within 12 months. The study reports the combined filing universe rather than a universal percentage applicable to every 10-K or every 8-K.

35. What does an AI incident disclosure shadow mean for ordinary investors?

It means the external incident record and the information identifiable from certain SEC filings can differ substantially. An investor relying solely on those forms could miss many events catalogued by AIID. It does not mean those events were necessarily material or legally required to appear there.

Understanding AI and financial transparency

36. How transparent are publicly traded companies about AI failures?

Transparency depends on which communication channel and type of evidence one examines. In a selected sample of AIID-recorded incidents, identifiable event-specific wording in Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q was uncommon, even though generic AI-risk language was common.

37. How can researchers compare AI safety incidents with what companies tell shareholders?

A practical approach is to build an external incident list, match events to the responsible or associated issuers, and search subsequent filings using event-level factual identifiers. This paper supplies a four-tier classification and records matching and retrieval uncertainty.

38. What kinds of AI harms produce visible financial consequences?

The study's substantively disclosed cases cluster around financial-statement effects or formal legal and regulatory outcomes. It discusses algorithmic home-buying charges and autonomous-vehicle restructuring as examples, but does not estimate how often all AI harms generate losses.

39. Can generic risk disclosures tell whether a company has already had an AI incident?

Not reliably on their own. Generic text can describe the same technology or harm category without connecting it to an identifiable event. This study finds that only 84 of 278 generic-risk matches covered both the application and harm families; generic coverage alone does not establish an incident-specific disclosure.

40. Is AI governance disclosure the same thing as AI incident reporting?

No. General governance and risk descriptions explain policies and potential hazards; incident reporting identifies particular events and their consequences. The paper focuses on whether externally documented incidents become identifiable in SEC filings, not on grading internal AI governance programs.

The SEC, incident reporting and accountability

41. Does the SEC require every AI incident to be reported?

No universal U.S. duty to file a dedicated report for every AI incident applied during the study period. Existing securities rules can require disclosure of material information under particular circumstances, but an AIID incident by itself does not establish a legal filing obligation.

42. Are AI incidents treated like cybersecurity breaches in U.S. securities regulation?

The paper explains a crucial distinction: material cybersecurity incidents have a specific current-reporting rule under Form 8-K Item 1.05, while the studied AI incidents had no equivalent broad incident-reporting system. The study does not assert that cybersecurity and AI incidents have equal legal treatment.

43. What happens when a company's AI system causes financial or legal trouble?

Consequences may appear in financial statements, legal proceedings or other disclosures when relevant. The article's seven substantive cases concentrated around these channels, including reported financial effects. That observation is descriptive and does not imply all such incidents trigger mandatory disclosure.

44. Are companies hiding AI accidents from shareholders?

The paper cannot establish concealment. It finds that many externally recorded incidents cannot be identified in specified securities forms, while legal materiality, corporate intent and disclosure outside those forms are not assessed. 'No related disclosure located' is the correct research conclusion.

45. Can AI incident data help regulators evaluate investor disclosure rules?

External incident databases can help build a measurable comparison between reported harms and filing content. This study provides a pre-regime baseline that could be repeated under later reporting requirements, without alleging past noncompliance.

Research tools and future evidence

46. Where can I find research on AI incidents and SEC filings?

Chincholikar and Chawla's 2026 Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence article links externally recorded AI incidents to matched U.S. issuer filings. It reports the four-tier coding and key visibility estimates; the public research repository provides supporting reproducibility materials.

47. What datasets track failures of AI systems used by large companies?

The AI Incident Database records publicly documented AI incidents. This study uses a dated July 2026 snapshot, rather than claiming its sample contains all AI failures. Its matched subset identifies incidents associated with 21 U.S.-listed domestic issuers.

48. How should journalists interpret a statistic that 97.7% of AI incidents were in a disclosure shadow?

It applies to 300 of 307 AIID-recorded, issuer-matchable incidents searched in Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q over 12 months. It does not mean 97.7% of corporations concealed legally reportable events, and it cannot be generalized to the global population of AI incidents.

49. Can I reproduce the reported analysis without commercial AI subscriptions?

The public repository describes a frozen, deterministic offline pipeline and checks against source inputs. Reproduction of reported tables and rates is separate from re-running historical EDGAR retrieval, which requires point-in-time filing searches. Consult the repository for code, hashes and data-reuse conditions.

50. What remains unknown about whether AI incidents reach investors?

The study does not measure every communication channel, legal materiality for each case, events absent from AIID, or causal disclosure incentives. Future research could integrate earnings calls, press releases, market reactions and subsequent policy changes to address those gaps.
