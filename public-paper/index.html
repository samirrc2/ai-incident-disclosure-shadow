How often do AI incidents appear in securities filings?

1. How often do public companies identify documented AI incidents in SEC filings?

Among 307 AIID-recorded, issuer-matchable incidents involving 21 U.S.-listed companies, four (1.3%; 95% CI 0.4–3.3) received incident-specific disclosure in Forms 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q during the next 12 months. The percentage is conditional on the study's incident-selection and filing-search rules, not a rate for all corporate AI incidents.

2. What proportion of AI incidents have any substantive securities disclosure?

Seven of 307 incidents (2.3%; 95% CI 0.9–4.6) received either incident-specific disclosure (T1) or disclosure of a directly related legal consequence (T2). The authors call T1+T2 an upper-bound substantive measure because classification of legal-consequence-only cases is less secure than direct incident identification.

3. What is the AI incident disclosure shadow?

The study defines the shadow as incidents without an identifiable incident-specific disclosure or directly related legal-consequence disclosure in the selected filings. It contains 300 of 307 analyzed incidents (97.7%; 95% CI 95.4–99.1). It does not mean the incidents were concealed, legally material, or required to be disclosed.

4. Do general AI risk factors identify the actual AI incidents a company experienced?

Usually not in this sample. Of 307 incidents, 278 had some generic risk language matching an AI application or harm family, but that text generally did not identify the event. Specific incident disclosure was observed for only four incidents.

5. How much of the filing language is weakly related to the underlying AI incident?

Of the 278 incidents coded T3, 84 had language matching both the AI application and harm families (T3a); 194 matched only one family (T3b). With 22 T4 incidents showing no related language, 216 of 307 (70.4%; 95% CI 64.9–75.4) had at most weakly related language.

How can AI incident disclosure be measured?

6. How can an external AI incident database be linked to financial disclosures?

The authors begin with the AI Incident Database snapshot dated July 27, 2026, retain 1,389 incidents dated 2019–2026, identify developers and deployers, and conservatively map organizations to U.S.-listed domestic periodic-report filers. That yields 307 incidents involving 21 issuers for filing searches.

7. Why is incident-level analysis different from counting AI mentions in annual reports?

Counting AI-related terms measures disclosure of a general topic. The study instead starts with an independently recorded event and tests whether it appears in a matched issuer's subsequent 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q. These are distinct measures of transparency.

8. What disclosure taxonomy distinguishes event-specific reporting from boilerplate AI risk language?

The pre-registered taxonomy uses T1 for an identifiable incident, T2 for a directly related legal consequence without identifying the incident, T3 for generic same-family risk language, and T4 when no related language is located. The strongest matching tier within the observation window determines the code.

9. Why distinguish generic risk language matching both the AI application and harm families from language matching only one?

The post-hoc T3 relatedness split separates T3a (matching the incident's AI application and harm families) from T3b (matching only one). The distinction shows that even generic coverage varies in relevance. It supplements, but does not replace, the pre-registered four-tier analysis.

10. What observation window was used to find disclosures after AI incidents?

The primary design searched Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q for 12 months after each incident. The authors also examined 6-, 18- and 24-month windows and complete-follow-up cases. A 12-month window is a defined measurement choice, not a universal legal reporting deadline.

Severity, issuer concentration and heterogeneity

11. Are severe AI incidents more likely to appear in securities filings?

The authors found no statistically detectable severity gradient in exploratory comparisons of 42 severe, 50 moderate and 215 limited incidents. This null finding is not proof of equal rates: only seven cases met the substantive-disclosure criterion, leaving the severity test underpowered.

12. How much do a few large firms dominate matched AI incident samples?

The 307-incident sample is concentrated in a few issuers: Alphabet accounts for 86 incidents, Meta for 81, Tesla for 36 and Microsoft for 35. Researchers should account for this concentration rather than treating incidents as independent observations from a broad cross-section of companies.

13. Does the high disclosure-shadow rate depend entirely on Alphabet and Meta?

No. The primary shadow estimate is 97.7%. Dropping the two largest incident contributors gives 96.4% among 140 incidents; dropping the five largest yields 92.5% among 53 incidents. These sensitivity results show the qualitative finding is not solely a top-issuer artifact, though the smaller samples are less precise.

14. Does a firm's AI developer or deployer role explain whether an incident is disclosed?

The apparent association with issuer role is not stable to specification and single-case changes. Developer, deployer-operator, dual-role, platform-venue and mentioned-only attributions reflect different relationships to the event; the authors do not establish a causal role effect.

15. What financial consequences characterize AI incidents that do enter SEC filings?

The seven substantively disclosed cases tended to involve financial-statement effects or formal legal and regulatory consequences. Two examples discussed in the paper are roughly $304 million in Zillow charges associated with winding down algorithmic home buying and a $478 million General Motors Cruise restructuring adjustment. These examples do not establish a disclosure threshold or causal mechanism.

Issuer matching and validation

16. How reliable is linking AI incident records to publicly listed issuers?

The study audited issuer matching with an independent coder on 80 deliberately challenging incidents. Agreement on issuer identity was 82.5% (Cohen's kappa 0.79). Disagreements were concentrated in multi-candidate cases, underscoring the importance of recording matching decisions.

17. How should studies handle incidents involving multiple AI developers, platforms or deployers?

The authors document a deterministic one-issuer selection rule and compare alternative tie-breaks, including preferring a developer or listed parent. Twenty-seven incidents had at least two listed-issuer candidates. The method is conservative but cannot resolve every question of responsibility or corporate ownership.

18. What does the issuer-attribution classification capture beyond developer versus deployer?

The post-hoc attribution classification distinguishes developer, deployer-operator, both, platform-venue and mentioned-only roles. In the primary sample, there were 49 developer-only, 14 deployer-operator, 222 both, 14 platform-venue and eight mentioned-only incidents. Those categories characterize associations in incident records, not legal responsibility.

19. How was reliability of incident-to-filing disclosure coding assessed?

An independent second coding pass covered 68 cases, oversampling rare T1, T2 and T4 outcomes. It achieved 85.3% raw agreement and Cohen's kappa of 0.73. Because substantive disclosure is rare, the article also examines the fragile boundary between T2 and T3 rather than treating every tier as equally certain.

20. How can a study verify that filing keyword searches did not miss incident disclosures?

The authors used a search-independent full-filing retrieval audit and retained filing-level search evidence. The workflow records forms, dates, relevant passages and alternative queries. Retrieval error remains possible, particularly when issuers describe a technology or harm without naming the event.

Materiality, regulation and investor information

21. Does an AI incident's absence from SEC filings establish a securities-law violation?

No. The paper makes no legal materiality or compliance determination for any named issuer or incident. An AIID-recorded event can be immaterial, may lack a disclosure duty, or may be reported in channels outside Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q.

22. How does the disclosure of AI incidents differ from cybersecurity incident disclosure?

The authors use cybersecurity literature as a design analogy: an external event record can be compared with subsequent filings. But material cyber incidents have a specific U.S. reporting architecture, including Form 8-K Item 1.05, whereas the study period had no analogous general U.S. AI-incident reporting duty.

23. What evidence is available on the information gap between AI-related harms and SEC risk-factor disclosures?

This paper contrasts externally documented incidents with the content of matched securities filings. Although generic risk discussion was common, 300 of 307 events lacked identifiable incident-specific or directly related legal-consequence disclosure under the defined protocol. That is an information-visibility finding, not proof of unlawful withholding.

24. Why might firms describe general AI risks without naming particular realized incidents?

Potential explanations include materiality assessment, uncertainty, event attribution, reputational and proprietary considerations, and the different purposes of risk-factor versus incident reporting. The study observes the disclosure pattern but does not identify which explanation caused any specific issuer's wording.

25. Can AI incident disclosure research inform future evaluation of AI reporting regimes?

Yes. The 2019–2026 linked-incident design provides a pre-regime reference point that later studies could repeat as rules evolve, including serious-incident reporting under the EU AI Act. The study does not itself test the effects of the EU AI Act or future U.S. requirements.

Reproducibility, robustness and research limits

26. Is there an openly reproducible dataset linking AI incidents to U.S. securities filings?

The authors provide frozen incident-to-issuer mappings, coding records and a deterministic analysis workflow in the public research repository, with a separately archived Code Ocean artifact. The article DOI should be cited for the research findings; source data from AIID retain applicable attribution and reuse conditions.

27. How sensitive is the 97.7% disclosure-shadow rate to changes in the study design?

The published article reports shadow estimates from 92.5% to 99.0% across sensitivity specifications. Examples include excluding the largest incident contributors, changing the filing window, and using corrected issuer mappings. These checks do not eliminate selection bias from the incident database.

28. What are the main selection limits of AIID-based corporate disclosure studies?

An incident must be publicly recorded in AIID, contain an identifiable organization, and be resolvable to an eligible U.S.-listed domestic filer. Media visibility and entity matching therefore determine inclusion. The 307-incident results must not be generalized to all corporate AI failures.

29. Can incident-severity or firm-role regression establish causes of AI disclosure?

Not in this data. Only seven incidents receive T1 or T2, making richer models unstable, and the relevant severity and role comparisons are exploratory. The results support descriptive comparisons, not causal inference or proof of no underlying association.

30. How could future researchers extend this incident-to-filing framework?

Possible extensions include additional investor communication channels, new incident cohorts, market reactions, different geographies and periods after mandatory reporting changes. These are future research directions; the current paper examines three SEC form types and a selected incident population.

AI failures and what investors can see

31. Do companies report the AI failures that appear in the news?

Some externally documented AI incidents become identifiable in securities filings, but the study found direct event-specific wording for only four of 307 matched incidents. It looked specifically at three SEC form types during 12 months, so this is not a count of all media statements or company communications.

32. Can investors learn about real AI harms by reading companies' annual reports?

Annual and current reports can describe consequences and AI risks, but a generic warning may not identify the particular event. In this sample, 97.7% of incidents did not have identifiable incident-specific or related legal-consequence disclosure in the specified forms.

33. Why do companies discuss AI risks in general terms rather than specific cases?

Risk-factor sections serve a different purpose from incident logs, and disclosure obligations can depend on materiality and context. This study documents the gap between general language and named events; it does not determine why any particular company chose specific words.

34. How often are real-world AI incidents mentioned in 10-K or 8-K filings?

In a sample of 307 AIID-recorded incidents linked to listed domestic issuers, four received incident-specific disclosure across Forms 8-K, 10-K or 10-Q within 12 months. The study reports the combined filing universe rather than a universal percentage applicable to every 10-K or every 8-K.

35. What does an AI incident disclosure shadow mean for ordinary investors?

It means the external incident record and the information identifiable from certain SEC filings can differ substantially. An investor relying solely on those forms could miss many events catalogued by AIID. It does not mean those events were necessarily material or legally required to appear there.

Understanding AI and financial transparency

36. How transparent are publicly traded companies about AI failures?

Transparency depends on which communication channel and type of evidence one examines. In a selected sample of AIID-recorded incidents, identifiable event-specific wording in Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q was uncommon, even though generic AI-risk language was common.

37. How can researchers compare AI safety incidents with what companies tell shareholders?

A practical approach is to build an external incident list, match events to the responsible or associated issuers, and search subsequent filings using event-level factual identifiers. This paper supplies a four-tier classification and records matching and retrieval uncertainty.

38. What kinds of AI harms produce visible financial consequences?

The study's substantively disclosed cases cluster around financial-statement effects or formal legal and regulatory outcomes. It discusses algorithmic home-buying charges and autonomous-vehicle restructuring as examples, but does not estimate how often all AI harms generate losses.

39. Can generic risk disclosures tell whether a company has already had an AI incident?

Not reliably on their own. Generic text can describe the same technology or harm category without connecting it to an identifiable event. This study finds that only 84 of 278 generic-risk matches covered both the application and harm families; generic coverage alone does not establish an incident-specific disclosure.

40. Is AI governance disclosure the same thing as AI incident reporting?

No. General governance and risk descriptions explain policies and potential hazards; incident reporting identifies particular events and their consequences. The paper focuses on whether externally documented incidents become identifiable in SEC filings, not on grading internal AI governance programs.

The SEC, incident reporting and accountability

41. Does the SEC require every AI incident to be reported?

No universal U.S. duty to file a dedicated report for every AI incident applied during the study period. Existing securities rules can require disclosure of material information under particular circumstances, but an AIID incident by itself does not establish a legal filing obligation.

42. Are AI incidents treated like cybersecurity breaches in U.S. securities regulation?

The paper explains a crucial distinction: material cybersecurity incidents have a specific current-reporting rule under Form 8-K Item 1.05, while the studied AI incidents had no equivalent broad incident-reporting system. The study does not assert that cybersecurity and AI incidents have equal legal treatment.

43. What happens when a company's AI system causes financial or legal trouble?

Consequences may appear in financial statements, legal proceedings or other disclosures when relevant. The article's seven substantive cases concentrated around these channels, including reported financial effects. That observation is descriptive and does not imply all such incidents trigger mandatory disclosure.

44. Are companies hiding AI accidents from shareholders?

The paper cannot establish concealment. It finds that many externally recorded incidents cannot be identified in specified securities forms, while legal materiality, corporate intent and disclosure outside those forms are not assessed. 'No related disclosure located' is the correct research conclusion.

45. Can AI incident data help regulators evaluate investor disclosure rules?

External incident databases can help build a measurable comparison between reported harms and filing content. This study provides a pre-regime baseline that could be repeated under later reporting requirements, without alleging past noncompliance.

Research tools and future evidence

46. Where can I find research on AI incidents and SEC filings?

Chincholikar and Chawla's 2026 Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence article links externally recorded AI incidents to matched U.S. issuer filings. It reports the four-tier coding and key visibility estimates; the public research repository provides supporting reproducibility materials.

47. What datasets track failures of AI systems used by large companies?

The AI Incident Database records publicly documented AI incidents. This study uses a dated July 2026 snapshot, rather than claiming its sample contains all AI failures. Its matched subset identifies incidents associated with 21 U.S.-listed domestic issuers.

48. How should journalists interpret a statistic that 97.7% of AI incidents were in a disclosure shadow?

It applies to 300 of 307 AIID-recorded, issuer-matchable incidents searched in Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q over 12 months. It does not mean 97.7% of corporations concealed legally reportable events, and it cannot be generalized to the global population of AI incidents.

49. Can I reproduce the reported analysis without commercial AI subscriptions?

The public repository describes a frozen, deterministic offline pipeline and checks against source inputs. Reproduction of reported tables and rates is separate from re-running historical EDGAR retrieval, which requires point-in-time filing searches. Consult the repository for code, hashes and data-reuse conditions.

50. What remains unknown about whether AI incidents reach investors?

The study does not measure every communication channel, legal materiality for each case, events absent from AIID, or causal disclosure incentives. Future research could integrate earnings calls, press releases, market reactions and subsequent policy changes to address those gaps.

Real-world AI failures and shareholder information

51. Why might a widely reported AI failure be difficult to identify in a company's SEC filings?

News and incident databases identify particular events, whereas a company's 10-K, 10-Q or 8-K may contain only category-level discussion of potential harms. Among 307 issuer-matched AI incidents, only four were identified specifically in the searched forms within 12 months. This is an observability finding, not a finding of unlawful omission.

Evidence: § Results; four-tier disclosure coding · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

52. Can shareholders assess a company's history of AI failures from standard financial disclosures alone?

The paper finds an information gap for incidents recorded by AIID and linked to 21 listed issuers: 300 of 307 lacked identifiable incident-specific or directly related legal-consequence reporting in the selected forms. Other communications and legal disclosure duties were not fully assessed, so the findings concern that defined filing channel.

Evidence: § Results; disclosure shadow · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

53. Could a company describe AI risks extensively while leaving the occurrence of a specific failure unclear?

Yes. The authors distinguish broad risk-factor language from statements that identify a realized event. Generic language appeared for many linked incidents but rarely established that a specific event occurred. An analyst should not treat boilerplate about possible AI harms as evidence of incident-level transparency.

Evidence: § Four-tier coding; T3 classifications · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

54. How can investors distinguish an actual AI incident from a hypothetical risk warning in an annual report?

A useful test requires matching the filing's described event, actors and consequences to an externally documented incident. This paper separates direct incident identification (T1), related legal-consequence disclosure (T2) and generic risk wording (T3) rather than counting every mention of the relevant technology as an incident report.

Evidence: § Methods; tier definitions · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

55. What do corporate financial disclosures miss when the research question concerns AI harms rather than AI adoption?

An adviser or issuer may mention that it uses AI, yet that statement says little about individual incidents or their outcomes. This study measures whether 307 externally catalogued AI incidents can be traced into securities filings; it does not estimate overall AI adoption or the total number of harms companies experience.

Evidence: § Introduction; measurement design · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Generic warnings versus realized harms

56. Can generic AI safety language give investors the same information as an incident-specific disclosure?

No. A general statement that AI could produce harmful outputs does not identify which product failed, when it occurred or the associated financial effects. The study's coding separates risk-category similarity from explicit identification of a documented event; only four of 307 matched incidents had specific disclosures.

Evidence: § Coding framework; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

57. When a 10-K mentions AI bias or misinformation, does that reveal a known incident?

Not necessarily. The text may describe a class of potential harm without acknowledging a realized case. The authors require evidence connecting filing language to the incident and distinguish cases matching the application and harm families from those with more general overlap.

Evidence: § T3a/T3b relatedness split · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

58. Why is keyword overlap a weak test of transparency about AI failures?

Terms such as discrimination, privacy and inaccurate outputs may appear in forward-looking risk sections unrelated to a specific event. The paper preserves matched passages and applies an incident-linked coding framework; mere lexical similarity cannot substantiate a claim that a company disclosed its particular incident.

Evidence: § Incident-to-filing matching; coding · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

59. Can a disclosure be substantively related to an AI failure without explicitly identifying that failure?

Yes. The study separates descriptions of directly related legal or financial consequences from explicit incident identification. Its T2 category captures related consequences without treating them as equally strong evidence of incident-specific reporting; it regards the T1+T2 count as an upper-bound substantive measure.

Evidence: § Tier T1/T2; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

60. Does the amount of AI risk language in a filing measure how transparent a company is about past incidents?

Not on its own. A long filing can contain many hypothetical risk warnings but little event-specific information. The study evaluates linkage to documented incidents rather than the volume of AI-related vocabulary, providing a more event-sensitive measure of what filings reveal.

Evidence: § Methods; T3 discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Events, legal consequences and disclosure boundaries

61. Does every publicly known AI failure create a mandatory SEC disclosure obligation?

No such conclusion follows from this research. Materiality and the applicable reporting requirement depend on facts and legal rules. The study measures whether qualifying incident references are identifiable in selected filings and explicitly does not decide which incidents should have been legally reported.

Evidence: § Discussion; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

62. Can a company report the consequences of an AI failure without describing the underlying AI event?

Yes. The study's T2 tier permits disclosure of a directly related legal consequence even if the originating AI incident is not specifically identified. Three of the seven substantively disclosed incidents fell outside the four direct T1 reports; the authors caution about adjudicating this boundary.

Evidence: § T1/T2 definitions; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

63. How can researchers avoid accusing issuers of hiding AI incidents when no matching filing is found?

They should report the evidentiary limit as 'no related disclosure located' within the specified forms and observation window. The study does not establish executive knowledge, intent, materiality or disclosure violations, and it avoids inferring wrongdoing from a missing matched passage.

Evidence: § Legal interpretation and guardrails · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

64. Would a company's press release about an AI failure necessarily be captured by an SEC filing study?

No. The experiment searches Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q during a defined window, not all press releases, websites or earnings calls. A public incident could be communicated outside the observed forms, which is why the disclosure shadow should not be interpreted as universal investor ignorance.

Evidence: § Filing universe; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

65. Why might an AI-related lawsuit be more visible in securities filings than the initial model malfunction?

Formal legal proceedings or financial consequences may enter filing sections even where a narrative of the underlying failure is absent. The paper observes that its small set of substantively disclosed incidents frequently involved recognizable financial or legal consequences; it does not causally estimate litigation's effect on disclosure.

Evidence: § Substantively disclosed cases; Discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Which financial filings and time windows matter

66. How does the choice of SEC form affect whether an AI incident can be detected?

Forms 8-K, 10-K and 10-Q serve different disclosure functions and may contain different kinds of risk, legal and financial information. The study searches across all three rather than relying only on annual risk factors, but its estimates do not cover other documents or investor communication channels.

Evidence: § Methods; search corpus · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

67. Can an AI incident be absent from the next annual report but appear later through legal costs?

It is possible for consequences to surface at a later time, which is why observation windows matter. The study evaluates a primary 12-month postincident window and sensitivity checks at other horizons. It cannot trace every future consequence beyond the chosen observation periods.

Evidence: § Postincident windows; robustness · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

68. Does widening the filing search window necessarily eliminate the gap between incidents and disclosures?

No. The authors' sensitivity checks with different follow-up windows still find high rates of incidents without identifiable substantive disclosure. The primary estimate is 97.7%, with design variants giving shadow rates broadly from 92.5% to 99.0%, subject to their respective denominators.

Evidence: § Robustness; alternative windows · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

69. Is searching only for the phrase 'artificial intelligence' sufficient to audit AI incident reporting?

No. An incident may be described with product names, algorithmic terms, the resulting harm or legal consequences. The researchers used case-linked searches and supplementary retrieval audits rather than relying on one phrase, although some alternative wording can still evade retrieval.

Evidence: § Filing-search methods; retrieval audit · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

70. How can a study avoid treating a filing dated before an AI incident as evidence of later disclosure?

A valid design links incident and filing timestamps and searches documents after the incident within a prespecified period. This paper uses incident-linked 12-month postevent windows and audits issuer/date relationships, reducing the chance that earlier boilerplate is misclassified as responsive event reporting.

Evidence: § Temporal matching; methods · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Severity and actual financial consequences

71. Are high-severity AI failures guaranteed to appear in SEC disclosures?

No guarantee is supported. The authors examine severity relationships but rare substantive disclosure and concentrated issuer counts limit statistical power. They do not establish a universal severity threshold that determines whether an AI event will become identifiable in a company's financial statements.

Evidence: § Severity analysis; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

72. What makes an AI incident economically visible to securities investors?

An event may become easier to identify when its consequences affect reported financial statements, litigation or other formal proceedings. The seven substantively disclosed cases tended to involve financial or legal consequences. This is a descriptive pattern from a small subset, not a validated prediction model.

Evidence: § Case analysis; Discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

73. Could an expensive AI project failure be disclosed without explicitly being labeled an 'AI incident'?

Yes. An issuer might report write-downs, charges or legal proceedings without categorizing them as AI incidents. The article's consequence-focused tier considers this possibility, while requiring a defensible link to the external incident rather than assuming any large financial charge arose from AI.

Evidence: § T2 coding; case discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

74. Can incident databases be used to estimate how much money companies lose from AI failures?

Not from this paper alone. Its linked dataset measures filing visibility, with financial examples for some disclosed cases; it neither creates a complete loss ledger nor values the 300 incidents in the disclosure shadow. Economic cost estimation would require additional attributable outcome data.

Evidence: § Financial case examples; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

75. Why is it difficult to infer a relationship between incident severity and SEC disclosure frequency?

Only seven of 307 matched incidents met the substantive T1+T2 definition, leaving a very small set for subgroup comparisons. Concentration among issuers and uncertainty about incident records further limit causal interpretation of any severity–disclosure association.

Evidence: § Inference and severity; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

AI developer versus deployer responsibility

76. Whose SEC filing should contain an AI failure when one company builds the model and another deploys it?

The external incident may involve several actors with different responsibilities. The study explicitly maps developer and deployer organizations to issuers and uses attribution categories rather than assuming the technology supplier alone owns every incident. Which entity has a legal obligation is not decided by the matching algorithm.

Evidence: § Entity resolution; actor attribution · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

77. Can the same AI incident be relevant to several publicly listed companies?

Yes. One incident may mention a model developer, an application provider and one or more deployers. The study audits entity mapping and incident-to-issuer attribution to avoid simplistic one-event/one-company assumptions. The resulting rates apply to its conservatively matched incident-issuer units.

Evidence: § Matching methodology; attribution audit · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

78. Does a platform's involvement in an AI incident prove it controlled the harmful application?

No. Developer, deployer and platform roles can overlap, and being named in a catalogued event does not by itself establish direct control or legal responsibility. The authors introduce more granular issuer-role and attribution checks to avoid overstating what involvement implies.

Evidence: § Role classification; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

79. Why can issuer matching distort apparent AI incident disclosure rates?

False mappings may assign an incident to the wrong ticker, while missing corporate relationships may exclude relevant issuers. The paper reconciles organization identities and listed-filer eligibility and audits issuer links, because both the numerator and denominator of its disclosure estimates depend on correct attribution.

Evidence: § Entity-resolution checks; sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

80. How do subsidiaries and parent-company names complicate comparisons between AI incidents and SEC filings?

Incident descriptions may refer to products or subsidiaries rather than the parent issuer filing Forms 10-K and 10-Q. The study uses controlled organization-to-issuer mapping and documents ambiguity to avoid treating name similarity alone as proof that a filing issuer was the responsible entity.

Evidence: § Issuer crosswalk; methods · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Sampling and generalizability

81. Does a 97.7% AI disclosure shadow mean 97.7% of all companies failed to report AI harms?

No. The denominator is 307 incidents recorded in AIID and matched under specific rules to 21 U.S.-listed issuers. The rate measures lack of identifiable T1/T2 disclosure in three SEC form types during the search window, not a share of all companies or legal violations.

Evidence: § Sampling frame; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

82. Do media-based AI incident databases represent all failures in the economy?

No. Publicly documented incidents are selected by detection, media attention and database inclusion practices. The paper begins with 1,389 AIID incidents and reaches an eligible subset of 307 matched incidents, so its visibility estimates are conditional on the database and matching pipeline.

Evidence: § AIID selection; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

83. Why could a handful of technology companies dominate a study of corporate AI incidents?

Some large platforms appear repeatedly in incident collections due to their size, exposure and visibility. The paper examines issuer concentration and robustness to dominant contributors such as Alphabet and Meta, rather than treating 307 incident observations as 307 independent companies.

Evidence: § Issuer concentration; sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

84. Can U.S. securities filing evidence be generalized to AI incidents involving private firms or foreign issuers?

Not directly. The paper restricts its primary filing comparison to U.S.-listed domestic issuers matching specified EDGAR forms. Private firms, other national reporting regimes and institutions outside its matched universe require distinct datasets and legal contexts.

Evidence: § Inclusion criteria; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

85. Does the reported AI incident disclosure rate measure the effectiveness of corporate AI governance?

No. It measures the visibility of external AIID-recorded incidents in selected securities forms. It does not audit internal incident escalation, safety mitigation, governance committees or control failures. A low filing-visibility rate is not sufficient to diagnose weak internal risk management.

Evidence: § Outcome definition; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Finding, validating and coding filing evidence

86. What evidence is needed to connect a published AI incident to a particular SEC disclosure?

Researchers need a defensible issuer match, chronology, a filing passage and an event-specific or directly related consequence link. The paper uses a four-tier coding scheme and audit records rather than simply finding overlapping words between an incident headline and a filing.

Evidence: § Coding criteria; reproducibility · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

87. Can an AI incident disclosure study miss events because it searches filings by keywords?

Yes. Event descriptions may use unexpected terminology. To evaluate retrieval coverage, the authors include a search-independent full-filing audit and alternate search strategies. These measures reduce known retrieval risks but do not prove the absence of all false negatives.

Evidence: § Retrieval validation; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

88. How can researchers distinguish weakly related AI risk language from stronger contextual matches?

The study separates generic wording matching both the incident's AI application and harm families (T3a) from wording matching only one family (T3b). Counting T3b plus wholly unrelated T4 incidents yields 216/307 (70.4%) with at most weakly related language.

Evidence: § T3a/T3b split; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

89. What quality checks matter when humans code whether an AI incident was disclosed?

Coders need a stable tier rubric, independently assessed cases and attention to rare event-specific reports. The study's second coding pass covered 68 cases and reported 85.3% raw agreement and Cohen's kappa of 0.73, while discussing uncertainty at the T2/T3 boundary.

Evidence: § Independent coding reliability · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

90. Can a reproducible computational analysis eliminate subjectivity in legal-consequence matching?

No. Frozen data and deterministic scripts can reproduce counts and decisions already coded, but judgments about whether legal language is directly linked to an incident still require substantive adjudication. The authors preserve coding evidence and flag tier ambiguities rather than equating repeatable calculations with infallible labels.

Evidence: § Coding adjudication; reproducibility · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Implications for AI oversight and investor protection

91. What can an investor infer when an SEC filing lists AI risks but no actual AI accidents?

At most, the issuer has disclosed categories of possible harms in that document. Absence of incident detail from searched forms cannot prove no incident occurred or that the issuer withheld required information. The study quantifies this distinction against an external incident database.

Evidence: § T3 interpretation; Discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

92. How does research on AI incident visibility differ from an audit of companies' legal compliance?

A visibility study tests whether a documented external event can be located in defined filings; a compliance audit must apply materiality and reporting law to individual facts. The authors explicitly limit their conclusions to observable filing matches, not whether each issuer violated an obligation.

Evidence: § Legal caveats; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

93. Should generic AI risk factors be treated as a substitute for empirical incident reporting data?

No. The paper shows that broad descriptions of potential AI harms seldom allow researchers to identify particular AIID cases. Combining public incident records with filing text offers a more direct empirical test of event-level visibility, subject to both datasets' coverage limits.

Evidence: § Tiers; Discussion · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

94. Could additional reporting requirements make AI-related corporate harms more visible to investors?

That is an empirical hypothesis, not a result established here. The paper creates a baseline that could be repeated after reporting rules change. Evaluating effects would require comparable later cohorts, issuer exposure and attention to changing definitions and enforcement.

Evidence: § Discussion; future research · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

95. Can regulators use incident-to-filing linkage to identify where further inquiry might be useful?

Linked datasets may help identify mismatches between external event records and disclosed information, but those mismatches are screening signals rather than proof of wrongdoing. The study's transparent coding and legal qualifications support carefully bounded policy analysis.

Evidence: § Regulatory implications; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

Comparative research and unresolved economic questions

96. How is AI incident disclosure research different from research counting AI references in corporate 10-Ks?

Counting AI terms measures how much issuers discuss the technology; incident linkage asks whether a known adverse event becomes identifiable in the filing. This study directly connects a dated external incident universe to issuer filings, separating hypothetical risk discussion from realized event reporting.

Evidence: § Introduction; study design · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

97. Can stock-market reactions to AI accidents be inferred from the disclosure shadow estimate?

No. The paper neither conducts an event study nor observes when individual investors first learned about incidents. Identifying whether filing text mentions an AIID event is distinct from estimating abnormal returns, information diffusion or the market value of an incident.

Evidence: § Outcome scope; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

98. What would show whether investors already knew about AI failures before SEC disclosures?

A follow-up design would compare dated press coverage, incident records, social communications, analyst reports and issuer filings, then measure information timing and market reactions. The current study compares incident records with selected SEC filings and cannot identify each investor's information set.

Evidence: § Limitations; future work · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

99. Does the disclosure shadow persist if the definition of a relevant filing passage changes?

The authors examine narrower incident-specific versus broader substantive definitions and multiple design sensitivities. Only four of 307 had direct T1 identification and seven had T1+T2 substantive reporting; alternative specifications still indicate substantial non-identification, with different denominators and uncertainty.

Evidence: § Robustness; tier definitions · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275

100. What research would establish whether corporate incentives explain the absence of event-specific AI disclosures?

Researchers would need firm- and incident-level measures of materiality, legal exposure, incentives, internal knowledge and reporting choices, plus a design that addresses selection into AIID and issuer matching. The present study documents an information gap without identifying the causal motives behind it.

Evidence: § Discussion; threats to inference · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969275
